# Pedagogical Readiness — Round 1

**Recommendation:** needs revision
The document's conceptual spine is sound but the tutorial's stated centerpiece is a placeholder, one promised capability is only partially delivered, and the concrete examples assume a platform background the declared audience may not share.

**Audience read against:** Graduate STEM students who have worked with individual LLMs and agents but have not designed or coordinated multi-agent systems. Coding backgrounds vary; code should be followable but is not the primary teaching vehicle.

## Strengths

- The four-section arc — define, motivate, teach, close — matches how this audience thinks. A reader who has only used single agents has a clear ramp in at the right level.
- The codebase-audit failure example in `02-motivation.prose.md` is the best teaching moment in the document. The failure is concrete, the silence of the degradation is genuinely surprising, and the three-subagent fix is immediate. This example will stick with the audience.
- Putting the mental model before any mechanics in `03-content.prose.md` is the right call for a mixed-background audience. A reader who holds "orchestrator owns the problem, workers own the pieces" can follow the code snippet without reading code — they already know what it is doing.
- The capability list in `04-conclusion.prose.md` matches `01-context.prose.md` exactly. That discipline closes a loop the audience feels even if they cannot name it, and it means the tutorial does not promise things and quietly drop them.
- The pitfalls section names five real failure modes with causes, not just warnings. That is the right register for this audience — they are building systems, not passing a quiz.

## Weaknesses

- `03-content.prose.md` § *Live demo* — The demo is described in two separate spine documents as "the centerpiece" and "the primary hands-on moment." The prose contains an italic placeholder and three bullets listing what will happen. There is no scaffolding: no description of what the audience should watch for, what the orchestrator will actually do, or what output demonstrates the subagent/team distinction in practice. For a 15–18 minute tutorial where the demo is roughly a third of the time, the most important teaching moment is missing from the document. The clarity reviewer flags this as polish; from a readiness standpoint it is a harder failure — a presenter handed this document has nothing to work from, and a reader reviewing the site afterward has no record of what the demo showed.

- `03-content.prose.md` § *Subagents: the basic case* and *Teams: going further* — Both concrete examples are Claude Code–specific syntax (`Agent({...})`, "agent teams are enabled," task lists as a platform feature). The declared audience spans STEM disciplines with varying coding backgrounds; the spine justifies this with "it is the platform the audience uses," but the metadata audience declaration does not say that — it says graduate students across the sciences with hands-on LLM experience. A life-sciences or engineering PhD who interacts with LLMs via API or a UI interface has no grounding for these mechanics. The code is short enough to be followable in form, but the platform context it assumes is never established. One sentence of grounding ("Claude Code is the platform used in this course; these examples run there") would close the gap.

- `03-content.prose.md` broadly — The third promised capability is "Design a multi-agent system by decomposing a complex task into roles and workstreams." The document teaches what the roles are and shows a decomposition in passing (security / performance / coverage in the motivation example), but never teaches the reader *how* to decompose. The mental model describes structure; it does not supply a method. The conclusion restates the capability as delivered, but a reader finishing this tutorial could not, from the prose alone, walk up to a new task and reason through how to split it. The codebase-audit decomposition in section 02 does this implicitly — surfacing it as a teachable method in section 03 would earn the promise.

## Actionable

1. `03-content.prose.md` § *Live demo* — Replace the three-bullet placeholder with a short framing passage (3–5 sentences) stating what task the demo will run, what the audience should watch for (specifically: where the subagents' contexts do not appear in the orchestrator's conversation), and what the demo will show about the subagents-vs-teams distinction. Then keep or expand the bullet outline as the run-of-show. (*Why:* this is the most direct path from "needs revision" to "minor polish" — the demo is the primary teaching moment and it currently has no instructional content. A presenter can improvise a demo; the document cannot.)

2. `03-content.prose.md` § *Subagents: the basic case* — Add one sentence before the first code snippet establishing that the examples run in Claude Code and that the `Agent` tool is how Claude Code's orchestrator delegates work. (*Why:* the declared audience includes non-CS STEM readers; without this sentence the snippet appears as unexplained syntax. One sentence of grounding makes the example followable for the full audience range without adding load for readers already familiar with the platform.)

3. `03-content.prose.md` § *The mental model* or a new `##` subsection — Add a worked decomposition: take one concrete task (the codebase audit from section 02 is already in the reader's head) and show, in 4–6 sentences, the reasoning that produces the role split. What question does the orchestrator ask? What makes security, performance, and coverage the right three workers rather than some other partition? This is the "design" capability the proposal promises and the document does not yet deliver. (*Why:* the capability is restated as delivered in the conclusion, but the prose does not earn it — a reader cannot do this from the mental model alone. Surfacing the reasoning from the existing example costs little and closes a promise.)

4. `03-content.prose.md` § *Teams: going further* — Define **task list** before it appears as a feature of the spawn example. Currently the term arrives after the em-dash as a subordinate clause; the comparison table then uses it without re-defining it. Move the definition one paragraph earlier and bold it at first use. (*Why:* this is the clarity reviewer's finding, but it also has pedagogical weight — "task list" is new vocabulary for this audience and appears in the section's decision table, which functions as a reference. Jargon in a reference table that is not defined in the surrounding prose breaks the table for readers who use it after the fact.*)
